**Back in the “D”**

When my mother came to get me, she was wearing a beautiful sundress. It was black with big red toucans on it. I remember the beautiful colors and the bright yellow beaks of the birds. I was too young to understand seasons and I had just left a place that was scorching hot all year round and my concept of time hadn’t quite developed yet. My little mind could only conceptualize that it was cooler and I felt like we weren’t in Kansas anymore. People moved faster there and seemed like they were in a hurry. There were more people and people were less friendly. In the islands nobody’s in a hurry to get anywhere, I have a great understanding of *island time*. At such a young age it’s hard to grasp that I had no choice in any of what was happening in my life at that point. In my mind, the grown-ups knew best and they were the authorities. Surely they would make sure that I was safe, loved, cared for, and appreciated.

We drove from the airport back to Detroit and now it was just my mother and I. I’m not sure how long the drive took that part is all a blur but I do remember being reunited with my brother and sister. They seem so happy to see me. They stared at me and I was the center of attention, I remember my brother Larry laughing at me all the time because he said I talked funny. I was a little brownberry girl with an island accent. My skin had definitely been touched by the sun, and I didn’t know anything about colorism yet. I had the brief privilege of not being exposed to it yet. The days seemed longer and darker. The house was much quieter than when I was on the islands. There were always kids around, sunlight, and adults monitoring us. In Detroit, we were left alone while Mama went to work. She worked three jobs. I’m an 80s baby so there wasn’t a whole lot of talk about afterschool programs or daycare, it was big brother and sister where the babysitters or grandma or aunties house. There were fewer male father figures too. My mother worked as a secretary, at the Goodwill part-time and a GM Motors. How she had time for it all I don’t know. 

My sister was the one taking care of me the most. She made sure that I had my bath, my dinner was cooked, that I was dressed and she even braided my hair. My brother was a lot older than me, 9 years to be exact and I shared a room and bed with my sister so we were closer. I really looked up to her. I remembered we were much quieter than at the other house in St. Croix. I missed my dad and my cousins, but I was happy to have my brother and sister back too. At this point, I was six years old, I don’t ever remember going to kindergarten. I’m guessing I went in the islands, if I even went at all. I just remember the tail end of first grade, during this time my brother and sister, and I all rode the city bus downtown to go to school. It was a K-8 school.

* * *

I’ll never forget the time I had this encounter with this tomboy bully in school. Back in the 80s that’s what it was called when a girl dressed like a boy and played like the boys. I’ll never forget her name, her name was Shaunte she wore cornrows that went straight back, like Queen Latifah in the movie *Set it Off* she wore masculine clothes like her too, and she hung around with her older brother who was also a bully, about my brother’s age. Shaunte would pick on me, call me names, and really antagonize me. She scared me! I was a shy kid and very internal, always listening to my inner voice and feeling most comfortable alone and with people who were familiar to me. This girl was rough with her words and her ways, and though my early emotional beginnings were turbulent, I hadn’t encountered any bullies yet in my peer group. She was the first.

**The Challenge**

Shaunte continued to terrorize me for the entire school year. I would see Shaunte waiting for the bus on the ride home. “Every day.” Every day I dreaded when the school bell rang, completing another day of Westernized socialization and academics and the kickstart of the showdown that was about to happen between me and this girl. It was about to be *ON* at this bus stop. This particular day my brother told me “You’re gonna have to stop being afraid of this girl and you’re gonna have to stand up for yourself or you’ll always let people push over you!” That’s like an unspoken code in Detroit, no matter how scared you are you can never let anybody punk you because if you do, you’ll always be somebody’s punk. it was the last day of school and I remember having butterflies in my stomach the whole day because I knew that I was gonna have to stand up to Shaunte so I went outside to the bus stop and she started in with her mouth as usual, and this time I say something sassy back to her. 

Prior to that, I would pretend I didn’t hear her or that her words didn’t bother me very much but since that yielded no results other than her behavior escalating I figured I’d try my brother‘s approach for a change of pace. I decided this time I would stand up for myself, and I finally said something back to her. I don’t remember the words exactly but whatever it was she had a look of shock on her face, bewildered to be exact. She had absolutely no comeback! All of those days of the school year I had been her verbal punching bag and amusement with her brother instigating the whole thing. After I punked her publicly, her brother instructed her to hit me. She hit me and I swung and missed and then pushed her and she rolled down the hill. I was completely uncoordinated and unprepared for this event in my life. My brother and sister sat there and watched because I think everybody knew it was time to put this to an end once and for all. Saved by the bus, the bus driver rolled up just in time, my brother and sister and I hopped on, and low and behold. The girl's mother was on the bus and witnessed everything. 

When we got on the bus, their mother had them sit down, and they immediately started to try to explain to her frantically that I was the one that hit her. The mother said, “Be quiet, I saw everything!” She started talking to my brother to get the details and assured me they wouldn’t be a problem anymore. They were quiet and nervous. I felt a sense of pride because, for the first time, I stood up for myself. Lord knows I couldn’t fight but I tried. I mean, how do you even prepare a child to defend themselves at such an early age? Is that even a thing? I never saw Shaunte again. It’s amazing how little experiences like that begin to shape our reality. I had no idea, I’d learn to fight and survive in so many ways after that. Who knows what those kids had to encounter at home and what they saw and experienced as learned behavior that they practiced on me? All I knew was life sure was different in the States and at a young age I had begun to see violence as a normal part of life starting in my own home.

I was back in the States for about six months and I started to notice the vibe off there too. My dad never came back and my mother was a single mother working three jobs and we still were barely making ends meet. Being a single mother myself for a period of my life, I could only imagine the overwhelming stress it must’ve been to raise three children alone. I remember my brother and sister and I we played normally, but they were closer in age so they were always communicating with each other more. I would create my own inner world, almost as an only child sometimes. I liked it that way. 

I started noticing some patterns in our house that were slightly off. When my mother wasn’t around my brother and sister and I were like regular children when my mother came home there was fear in the air. We would scatter and make ourselves busy with something to do usually out of the direct line of her presence. We would become quiet, if you came into the house, you wouldn’t even know that children lived there we were that quiet. We weren’t giggling anymore we were careful not to raise the volume of our voices too high and I always felt there was some underlying understanding between my brother and sister that they knew not to upset Mama. Looking back on that their behavior was very typical for abused children. I didn’t know what they had been experiencing in their young lives because I had a very different existence in the islands. In St. Croix we played and were like normal children, when the adults came around, we stayed the same. There was no need to put on a different persona. We followed guidance and instruction and were disciplined when necessary. At home in Detroit, we went through extreme preventative measures to avoid *any* discipline.

During this time, I remember a lot of Bible studies and a lot of going to church. I hated it, and I didn’t understand why we had to go more than the other kids did. Even though there were a lot of underlying things happening that would begin to shape my reality for the rest of my life, I still didn’t fully understand at a young age *“What the hell was going on?”*  I was experiencing a new normal, and trying to figure out my place in this vast world, in this family, amongst my peers, and in society. Nobody was ever explaining anything to me, and I was trying to make sense of it as a six-year-old. Soon, I would understand why my sister and we’re so quiet and so fearful of our mother, and that incident would change my life from that moment forward.
